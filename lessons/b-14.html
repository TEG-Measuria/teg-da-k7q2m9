Blue light: comfort only, never health claims

6 minutes · Lenses

Purpose

After this lesson you can answer a patient's question about blue light calmly and accurately, describe a blue-light-filtering lens as a comfort feature, and avoid any claim about eye health. It matters because blue light is in the news, patients ask about it often, and a wrong claim can mislead a patient and breach advertising and professional rules.

Worked example

Ms D, 33, a graphic designer, says, "I have read that blue light from my screen is damaging my eyes. Do I need the blue light lenses?"

You do not say yes or no straight away. You say, "That is a common worry, so let us look at what we know." You explain: "Screens give out some blue light, but so does daylight, and much more of it. The current professional view is that there is no good evidence that the blue light from screens damages the eyes. What many people do find is that long hours on a screen make their eyes feel tired, and that is more about how much we focus and how little we blink than about colour. Some lenses filter a part of the blue light and some people say they feel more comfortable with them. That is a comfort choice, not a health one."

You then ask about her day, the screen distance, her breaks and her comfort. You suggest the 20 20 20 habit as a free tip: every 20 minutes, look 20 feet (about 6 metres) away for 20 seconds. She can choose a lens with a blue-light filter, an Eyezen design (b-03), or neither. You tell her the optometrist is the right person to ask about her eyes, and you note what you said.

The lesson

What blue light is. Visible light is made of colours, and blue is at the short-wavelength end. The sun is by far the biggest source. Screens and LED lighting give out a small amount.

What we can honestly say. The professional guidance, including that of the College of Optometrists, has been that there is no good evidence that blue light from screens harms the eyes, and that blue-light-filtering lenses have not been shown to prevent eye disease. What patients do report is tired, dry or uncomfortable eyes after long screen use. Lenses that filter some blue light, or lenses with a design for close work, may feel more comfortable to some people.

What we say, and what we never say.

  • We say: "may feel more comfortable on long screen days", "some people find", "it is a comfort option".
  • We never say: it protects your eyes, it prevents damage, it prevents macular problems, it stops eye strain for sure, or it is better for sleep. Those are health claims, and we do not make them.

What helps tired eyes, whatever the lens. Regular breaks, a screen at a comfortable distance and height, good lighting, blinking, and an up-to-date prescription. If a patient has dry or sore eyes, headaches or changes in vision, they should talk to the optometrist.

How to talk about the options. If a patient is interested, show the options and give the comfort language. Do not push a blue-light feature on someone who is not interested. If a patient asks if a feature is "needed", the honest answer is no: it is optional.

Related lessons. Eyezen is a screen-comfort design (b-03). A lens coating may reduce reflections that tire the eyes at night (b-11). For tint, see b-13.

Where your authority stops. You do not tell a patient whether their eyes are being harmed. You pass any health question to the optometrist.

Watch-outs

  • Never say blue light damages the eyes, or that a lens protects them from it.
  • Never say a lens helps sleep or prevents any disease.
  • Do not scare a patient to sell an option.
  • Do not dismiss the worry; explain calmly.
  • If unsure, ask a registrant.

Tips

  • Say "comfort" often.
  • Offer the 20 20 20 habit as a free tip.
  • Ask about breaks and screen distance as well as the lens.

Current practice notes

  • Blue light lens options are a comfort conversation; no eye-health claims are allowed.
  • The College of Optometrists' public guidance is the reference for what is and is not supported.
  • Essilor describes its blue-light-related lenses in terms of comfort; use their wording.

Drill

A "Mr H, 58" says, "Will blue light glasses stop me getting macular degeneration?" Answer in three sentences.

Show model answer

"I cannot say that, because there is no good evidence that blue light from screens or a lens that filters it prevents eye disease. Some people find a filter feels more comfortable on long screen days, so it is a comfort option, not a medical one. If you are worried about your eyes, the optometrist is the right person to ask, and I can book that for you."
