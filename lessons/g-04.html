Good, better, best: lens before price

8 minutes · The conversation

Purpose

You can present three lens options in a clear order, describing what each gives the patient before you say what it costs. It matters because patients choose well when they understand the difference, and the difference is hard to see once a price is in the air.

Worked example

Mr R, 38, software developer, has a new single vision prescription and spends his day at two screens. The dispenser has already found out his needs and made the link: tired eyes by the afternoon, and glare when he drives home.

She says: "I will show you three ways to do this. The first is a good clear lens with a hard coat. It sees well and it is a sensible choice. The second adds an anti-reflective coating, so there is less glare from screens and from headlights, and it is easier to keep clean. The third is a lens designed for screen work, with the coating as well, so your eyes do less work by the end of the day. For what you have told me, the optometrist's advice points to the third. Shall I give you the prices?"

Only then does she go to Price-it or the price list, and say the price of each. Mr R picks the middle one and says it makes sense. He understood what he was paying for.

The lesson

Lens before price

Show the lens before the price: describe the benefit first, then say what it costs. If the price comes first, the patient hears a number with nothing to set against it, and the cheapest option wins by default.

Three options

Offer three options: a good, a better and a best, and say which one the optometrist's advice points to.

  • Good: it works and it is a sensible choice. Never describe it as second-rate.
  • Better: it adds the most useful benefit for this patient.
  • Best: the gold standard for their needs, the lens you would choose with no budget limit.

Three is the right number. Two feels like a push or a pull. Four is confusing.

Say the difference in their terms

Between each option, say one clear difference in terms of the patient's day. "This one has less glare at night" is a difference. "This one has a different index" is not.

Lead with what fits

Lead with the option that fits their needs, not the cheapest or the dearest. If you start at the bottom, you set the patient's mind there. If you start at the top, say the gold standard and then explain that you can compromise.

Give the price of every option before anything is ordered

Give the price of each option before anything is ordered. The price comes from the group price list, per pair of lenses, by design, thickness and clear or light-reactive. Look it up in price-it; do not guess and do not say a figure from memory. Never override a price in the system.

Compromise, not downgrade

If the budget does not allow the best, say: "That is fine. Let us get you as close to it as we can." The aim is to keep the benefit that matters most to them.

Watch-outs

  • Do not say the price of the first option and then ask them to pick. They have only heard one number.
  • Do not describe the good option as basic, cheap or second-rate.
  • Do not let the best option become an automatic push. If it does not fit their needs, do not lead with it.
  • If a patient has said they have a fixed budget, take it seriously. See the lesson on trading up without pressure.
  • If a price looks wrong, ask. Staff cannot change a price in the system.

Tips

  • Say the three options in the same order each time so it becomes a habit.
  • Keep a line ready for the shortlist: "For what you have told me, I would choose the second."
  • Write the options and prices on the quote so the patient can take them home.

Current practice notes

  • One group price list applies at every practice: per pair, by design, index band and clear or Transitions. Use Price-it to look prices up.
  • Staff cannot override a price in Optix One (desktop); only Administrators can. If a price looks wrong, ask.
  • The conversation order (gold standard, budget, compromise) follows the Dispensing Conversation, Module 9.

Drill

Out loud: a patient who wears single vision for reading and driving. Describe good, better and best in three sentences without any prices, using one difference each.

Show model answer

"The first is a clear lens with a hard coat, a sensible choice that does the job. The second adds an anti-reflective coating, so less glare from headlights and screens. The third is the thinnest, lightest version with the coating, so the glasses are lighter on your nose as well." Then: "For what you have told me, I would choose the second. Shall I give you the prices?"

Today on the floor

Present three lens options (good, better, best) to a colleague in order, describing what each gives them before any price. Stop before you say a price. Swap roles. The colleague says which option they would choose and why.

About 4 minutes, with a colleague.

What your coach looks for

  • Describes the difference by what they would notice
  • Lens before price
  • Three clear steps, not a list of features
  • Lets the colleague choose; no pressure
