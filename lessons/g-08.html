Handling objections: the method

8 minutes · The conversation

Purpose

You can answer any objection with the same five steps, calmly and without pressure. It matters because a no is usually "not yet" or "I am not sure", and a patient who feels heard will often change their mind or come back.

Worked example

Mrs F, 55, shop manager, has heard the options and the price. She says, "That is a lot of money for glasses."

Weak: "Well, it's a good lens, and we have a discount at the moment." She has not been heard, and the dispenser has reached for an offer, which turns the answer into a pitch.

Strong:

  1. Acknowledge: "I completely get that. It is a real cost."
  2. Clarify: "Can I ask what the main thing is? Is it the whole pair, or one part of it?"
  3. Answer with the benefit to her: Mrs F says it is the lens upgrade. The dispenser says, "You told me you drive home after dark and the lights bother you. That is what this coating is for. If we leave it off, you keep the glare."
  4. Check: "Does that make sense?"
  5. Close: "Would you like to go ahead with it, or would you like to look at where we could compromise?"

Mrs F decides to keep the coating and choose a simpler frame. She was heard, so she decided.

The lesson

The five steps

Handle every objection the same way: acknowledge, clarify, answer with the benefit to them, check, close.

  1. Acknowledge. Agree with the feeling, not necessarily the facts. "I get that." "Of course." It lowers the temperature.
  2. Clarify. Find out what is really behind it. "What is the main thing holding you back?" The first thing a patient says is often not the real reason.
  3. Answer. Give the answer in terms of what the patient told you earlier, not general product facts. Use a short true story or a comparison if it helps.
  4. Check. "Does that make sense?" or "Is that helpful?" Stop and listen.
  5. Close. Offer the next step, with a middle option if the budget is the issue.

Acknowledge before you answer

Acknowledge first and answer second, because the patient cannot hear your answer until they feel heard. If you jump to the answer, it sounds like arguing.

Clarify once

An objection is often a question in disguise. "It is too expensive" can mean "I cannot afford it", "I am not sure it is worth it" or "I need to ask my partner". Each needs a different answer.

Never criticise another retailer

If the patient mentions another shop or an online seller, never criticise it. Compare what is included, not the headline price.

Ask once, never twice

After a no, offer one middle option, then let it go warmly. A patient who feels respected will come back. Write down what the optometrist advised and hold the quote.

Stories must be true

If you use a story, it must be true, with no names or details anyone could recognise. If you do not have one, say "A lot of patients tell me..." and never invent a story.

Stop when they hesitate

If a patient hesitates, goes quiet or asks a question, stop and answer it. Always leave one open door: "Take a moment, there is no rush."

Watch-outs

  • Do not offer a discount as your first answer. Offers are a sweetener, not the reason.
  • Do not argue, even when you are right.
  • Do not use fear to win the point.
  • Do not ask the closing question twice.
  • If an objection raises a clinical question, such as whether a lens is suitable, ask a registrant.

Tips

  • Write your own versions of the objection cards and practise them out loud.
  • Keep the clarify question open: what, how, tell me.
  • Smile when you acknowledge. It changes your voice.

Drill

With a colleague. They say, "I am not sure it is worth it." Respond with all five steps, one line each.

Show model answer

Acknowledge: "That is a fair question." Clarify: "What would make it feel worth it for you?" Answer: link to what they told you earlier. Check: "Does that make sense?" Close: "Shall we go ahead with this one, or look at a middle option?"

Today on the floor

A colleague picks any objection card and plays the patient. Use the five-step method: acknowledge, clarify, answer, check, close. Swap roles. The colleague ticks each step.

About 5 minutes, with a colleague.

What your coach looks for

  • Acknowledges before answering
  • Asks what is behind it
  • Answers using what the patient told you
  • Checks and closes once, voice falling, with no pressure
