The Dispensing Conversation: the shape of a great dispense

8 minutes · The conversation

Purpose

You can explain what makes a dispense great and name the four building blocks that every good conversation uses. It matters because a patient who understands why a lens suits them buys the right lens with confidence, and looks after their eyes better.

Worked example

Mrs K, 61, retired teacher, comes to the desk after her eye test. She says, "My eyes get so tired reading in the evenings." The dispenser hears a problem she wants to move away from.

The dispenser starts on good news: "Great news, the optometrist believes we can improve the comfort of your vision." That is a position block, because it sets the frame for what follows. Next she asks, "What does a normal evening look like for you?" Mrs K says she reads for two hours and does the crossword. That is a connect block, because it makes the advice about her.

The dispenser links the two: "What we normally advise for someone who reads as much as you do is a lens made for close work." Then she names the worry: "You may be thinking this sounds expensive. My job is to tell you what is best, and if the budget needs it, we will look at where to compromise." That is a reassure block. Finally she closes: "Are you happy to go with what the optometrist advised?" Her voice falls at the end, and she carries on towards the frames.

Nothing in that conversation was a trick. Each sentence did one job.

The lesson

Why people buy

People buy to move away from a problem, or towards something they want. Most patients are moving away from pain: tired eyes, headaches, blurred vision, a family history of eye disease. Some are moving towards pleasure: a new look, a new colour, a lens for a new hobby. Others want certainty ("the same frame as last time") or variety ("something different").

You find out which one by listening to the first thing they say. If you cannot tell, treat them as wanting to protect what they have, and lead with their health and the optometrist's advice.

The four building blocks

Every good conversation is built from four kinds of block: position, connect, reassure and close.

  • Position sets the frame. Start on good news, say what the optometrist advises, and present the advice as what you normally do.
  • Connect makes it about them. Tie the advice to their life, use a simple comparison they already understand, ask a small question.
  • Reassure takes the pressure off. Say their worry out loud before they do, give the gold standard, and offer to compromise if the budget needs it.
  • Close asks, then keeps moving as if it is agreed. You ask once and do not repeat the question.

You do not need to memorise scripts word for word. You are judged on results and on how patients feel, not on reciting.

Feature, benefit, meaning

A feature tells them what it is, a benefit tells them what it does, and the meaning tells them what it gives them. "Anti-reflective coating" is a feature. "Less glare at night" is a benefit. "Driving feels easier after dark" is the meaning. Spend most of your time on the meaning.

Why lenses come first

Lenses come first, because when money is tight people cut back on the frame before they cut back on the lenses. Once a lens is explained, it stops being an extra and becomes something the patient needs.

How you say it

Say "advises" rather than "we can offer", because a need sounds like a need and an offer sounds optional. Let your voice fall at the end of the close. A rising voice and a pause sounds like an invitation to say no.

Watch-outs

  • Do not recite. If a patient hesitates, goes quiet or asks a question, stop and answer it.
  • Only say "the optometrist advises" if it is on the record or you have just checked.
  • Do not promise that a lens will prevent or cure an eye condition. Say "look after your eyes".
  • Do not lead with an offer or a discount. Offers are a sweetener, never the reason.
  • If unsure what the optometrist advised, ask a registrant or check the record.

Tips

  • Pick one block from your own card and use it with your next patient.
  • Record yourself saying the close with a falling voice and listen back.
  • Repeat back something the patient told you. It shows you listened.

Drill

Out loud, to a colleague: a patient says, "I keep getting headaches at work." Say which driver this is, then say one sentence for each of the four blocks, in your own words.

Show model answer

This is moving away from pain. Position: "Great news, the optometrist believes we can improve the comfort of your vision." Connect: "What does a normal day at work look like for you?" Reassure: "My job is to tell you what is best, and if the budget needs it we will look at where to compromise." Close: "Are you happy to go with what the optometrist advised?" with the voice falling at the end.

Today on the floor

Role-play a short dispense with a colleague playing the patient: open, find out their day, link a lens, present options. Swap roles. The observer lists which of the four building blocks were used and which were missed.

About 4 minutes, with a colleague.

What your coach looks for

  • Starts with an open question
  • Listens more than speaks
  • Links the lens to something the patient said
  • Never pressures; suits the lens to the person
