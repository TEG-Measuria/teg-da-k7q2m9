Linking lifestyle to lens

7 minutes · The conversation

Purpose

You can turn what a patient told you about their life into one clear sentence that links it to a lens. It matters because a lens that is linked to a need feels like a solution; a lens described by its features feels like an extra cost.

Worked example

Mr T, 52, accountant. In needs-finding he said: "Eight hours at a screen, and by four o'clock my eyes are tired and I get a headache." He also drives home after dark.

Weak: "We have a lens with an anti-reflective coating and a blue light filter."

Strong: "You said your eyes are tired by four o'clock. What we normally advise for someone on screens all day is a lens designed for that kind of close work, so your eyes do less of the effort. And for the drive home, an anti-reflective coating cuts the glare from headlights, so it feels less tiring in the dark."

The strong version uses his words ("tired by four o'clock"), names one benefit per need, and does not mention a single technical term. The technical detail goes on the order, not into the conversation.

The lesson

One need, one lens, one sentence

Link one need to one lens, and say it in a single sentence. The shape is: what you told me, so what we normally advise, which means.

"You told me [their words], so what we normally advise is [lens], which means [meaning for them]."

Use their words

Use the patient's own words from needs-finding, so the link sounds like listening and not like selling. If they said "starry lights", say "starry lights". If they said "my eyes ache", say "ache".

Match the need to the lens family

  • Screens or close work all day: a lens designed for close and computer distance, so the whole desk is clear.
  • Night driving or glare: an anti-reflective coating; for frequent drivers, a lens designed for driving.
  • Outdoors in bright light: a lens that darkens outdoors, or a tinted or polarised second pair.
  • Scratches and wear: a hard, easy-clean coating.
  • Wearing glasses all day for several tasks: the best design the budget allows, because it covers more of their day.

Which product family is on the shelf is a matter for the lens lessons in the course and the price list. Here your job is to hear the need and name the benefit.

Use a comparison

A simple comparison gives them a line to repeat at home. For example, sun cream: a low factor is fine for popping in and out, a high factor for a long day outside. Keep it short and let them finish the saying themselves.

Watch-outs

  • Never promise a lens will prevent or cure an eye condition. Say "look after your eyes".
  • Blue light: talk about comfort only, never eye health.
  • Do not list features. Features belong on the order, not in the conversation.
  • Do not invent a need. If they did not mention driving, do not sell a driving lens.
  • If a patient has an eye condition, check the record for what the optometrist advised. If unsure, ask a registrant.

Tips

  • Say the benefit as something they will notice: "less glare", "a clearer desk", "fewer headaches by the afternoon" only if they said headaches.
  • Pause after the link and ask: "Does that make sense?"
  • Two links are plenty. Three starts to sound like a pitch.

Current practice notes

  • The lens handover (Module 9) shows the order: why it matters, the normal advice, a comparison, then the worry and the close.
  • Product benefits come from the manufacturer's public pages, restated in plain words; use the course product lessons for names.

Drill

A patient says: "I paint ceilings on ladders, and I do my accounts in the evening." Write one linking sentence for one need.

Show model answer

"You told me you spend your evenings on accounts, so what we normally advise for close work is a lens designed for that distance, which means a clearer page and less strain by the end of the evening." (Any sentence that uses their words, names one benefit and avoids technical terms is acceptable.)

Today on the floor

A colleague describes their day in a few sentences. Turn it into one sentence that links a lens or coating to a need: Because you do X, this does Y. Do it three times. They tell you if it sounded like a feature list.

About 3 minutes, with a colleague.

What your coach looks for

  • Uses the colleague's own words
  • One clear sentence, no jargon
  • Links to a real need, not an add-on
  • Stops and checks it fits
