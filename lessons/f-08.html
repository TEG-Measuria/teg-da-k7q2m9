Phone calls about glasses

7 minutes · Aftercare

Purpose

You can answer a phone call about glasses well: find out what is wrong, book the right slot, spot the warning words and hand over safely, without trying to diagnose over the phone.

Worked example

The phone rings. "Hello, [practice], [your name] speaking, how can I help?" It is Mr W, 55, electrician: "I picked up my new glasses on Monday and my head is pounding." You say, "I am sorry to hear that. Can I ask a few questions so I can help properly?" You ask which glasses, when he collected them, when the headache happens, and whether he sees double at any time. He says no double vision, but it is worse at the end of the day and the glasses feel tight. You open his file, book a 20-minute Spectacle Fitting slot for the next morning, and put the details in the remarks. You tell him to ring straight away if anything gets worse, if his vision changes suddenly, or if he sees double.

The lesson

A call about glasses is usually one of five things. Know what each one needs.

  1. Collection. Book a 20-minute dispensing or fitting slot. Note any special needs, for example "bringing own frames".
  2. Repair or adjustment. Ask exactly what is wrong. Book a 20-minute Spectacle Fitting slot and put the details in the remarks.
  3. Follow-up call (FUD). This is a 10-minute Phone Call to Patient slot at a quiet admin time. Put FUD and the notes in the remarks. Do not send a confirmation or reminder for FUD calls. Use FUD for first varifocals and for patients who said they will return later.
  4. Regular follow-up. Call patients who have recently collected glasses to ask how they are getting on. If they report blur or headaches, arrange a recheck or adjustment.
  5. A problem with new glasses. Listen, apologise, take details and book a recheck. Use the first-line non-tolerance steps when they come in.

Warning words. Sudden, loss of vision, pain, flashing lights, new floaters, a shadow or web, double vision, or a headache that is severe. If you hear any of these, treat it as urgent. Do not try to decide. Pass the call to a registrant straight away. If no registrant is on site, follow the practice emergency process: phone the practice's named registrant. If you cannot reach anyone, tell the patient this needs to be seen today: go to A&E or the eye casualty department now (not driving themselves), or call NHS 111. If vision loss comes with stroke signs (face drooping, arm weakness, slurred speech), call 999.

If you cannot answer, take contact details and promise a call-back rather than guessing. Keep that promise.

Delayed orders. Update the order status straight away, then phone the patient. If you cannot reach them, email as well.

Do not call the same person repeatedly in a short span. Log voicemails with the date.

Privacy. Confirm who you are speaking to before discussing a patient's details.

Watch-outs

  • Do not diagnose or reassure about symptoms you cannot examine.
  • Do not send confirmations or reminders for FUD calls.
  • Do not leave detailed messages on voicemail.
  • If you cannot answer, take contact details and promise a call-back.

Tips

  • Smile when you answer; it changes your voice.
  • Say your name and the practice name, then offer to help.
  • Write down the patient's words as they say them.

Current practice notes

  • Answer with who is speaking, which practice, and an offer of help.
  • Collections are 20-minute slots, FUD calls 10-minute slots, and repairs 20-minute Spectacle Fitting slots.
  • Check with your manager appointment lengths and booking screens in Optix One (desktop). Check with your practice manager.

Drill

A caller says she saw flashing lights this morning and her new glasses "feel odd". Say what you do first and what you do not do.

Show model answer

Treat "flashing lights" as a warning word: do not diagnose or reassure. Pass the call to a registrant straight away or follow the practice emergency process (the named registrant; if no one can be reached, A&E or eye casualty today, or NHS 111). Record everything in the remarks.

Today on the floor

Role-play a phone call with a colleague who rings about their glasses. Do it twice: once a simple repair booking, once where they use a warning word such as flashing lights. Show how you book the right slot and how you hand over the urgent one.

About 5 minutes, with a colleague.

What your coach looks for

  • Asks what is wrong and books the right slot with notes in the remarks
  • Confirms who you are speaking to before discussing details
  • Recognises the warning word and treats it as urgent
  • Promises a call back and does not diagnose
