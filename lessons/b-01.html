Lens types by need: single vision, office, bifocal, varifocal

7 minutes · Lenses

Purpose

After this lesson you can name the four basic kinds of spectacle lens, say in one sentence what each is for, and match a patient's day to the right kind. It matters because the wrong type of lens is the most common reason a pair comes back.

Worked example

Mr D, 56, a surveyor, has just been told by the optometrist that he needs help with close work for the first time. He drives to sites, reads plans on a desk and uses his phone all day. He asks, "Do I just need reading glasses?"

You ask three questions: where does he look most, how far away, and does he want one pair or more? He says plans on a desk about arm's length away, the phone, and the road. You write down three needs: far (driving), arm's length (desk) and near (phone, plans).

You explain the choices. Reading glasses give clear near vision but blur the distance, so he would take them on and off all day. A varifocal gives far, middle and near in one pair. A pair made for the desk gives a wide, comfortable view at near and arm's length, but it is not for walking about. He chooses a varifocal for everyday wear and you note that a desk pair may suit him later. You have not said a single product name yet, and that is deliberate: need first, product second.

The lesson

Every spectacle lens is built to put the eye's focus in the right place. What changes between the types is how many distances the lens covers.

Single vision has the same power across the whole lens. It corrects one distance: far, or near, or an in-between distance such as a computer screen. It is the simplest and the widest view, with nothing to learn when wearing it. Most people under about 40-45 need only single vision.

Office lenses (also called occupational lenses) are made for close and middle distances. They have a wide, comfortable view for a desk, a screen or a room, but the view of the far distance is limited or absent. They are a second pair for working in, not a pair for the road or the stairs. Our office lens is covered in b-04.

Bifocals have two powers in one lens: the main lens for distance and a visible segment at the bottom for near. The change between them is sudden and the line can be seen. There is nothing in between, so the middle distance can be blurred. Bifocals are covered in b-06.

Varifocals (progressives) have a smooth change in power from the top (distance) through the middle to the bottom (near), with no visible line. They give all three distances in one pair. The trade-off is that the sides of the lens have some blur, so the wearer learns to point their nose at what they want to see.

How to choose: begin with the patient's day, not the prescription. Ask what they do, how far away they look, whether they want one pair or several, and whether anything about their life makes a particular type unsafe (for example, a hobby that has them looking down a lot on stairs or ladders).

On who may sell what: the law does not restrict the sale of an adult's spectacles by lens design. An adult varifocal may be sold by an unregistered person against a prescription under two years old, provided the checks in the law are met. Under-16s and people registered sight impaired must be dispensed by or under the supervision of a registrant.

Watch-outs

  • Do not recommend a type from the prescription alone. Two patients with the same prescription may need different lenses.
  • Do not promise a first-time varifocal wearer that it will feel "normal" at once. Say that most people adapt in a week or two and book a check.
  • Never suggest an office lens as the only pair for someone who drives or climbs stairs with it on.
  • If unsure, ask a registrant. Under-16s and the registered sight impaired are always dispensed by or under a registrant.

Tips

  • Say "what do you spend your day looking at?" before you say a single product name.
  • Draw three horizontal lines on paper (far, middle, near) and tick which ones each lens type covers. Patients understand it in seconds.
  • Write the patient's needs on the dispense so the next person sees why a lens was chosen.

Current practice notes

  • The group sells adult spectacles against a valid prescription; the house rule is that under-16s and the registered sight impaired are dispensed by or under a registrant.

Drill

Mrs P, 62, is a bookkeeper who works at a screen all day, drives twice a week and likes to read in the evening. Say out loud, in plain words and without a product name, which type you would suggest for her everyday pair and which for her desk, and why.

Show model answer

A varifocal for everyday wear, because it gives far, middle and near in one pair with no line. A separate office lens (or a single vision pair for the screen) for her desk as a second pair, because it gives a wider, more comfortable view at screen and reading distance. You ask about her day before suggesting either, and you do not call the office pair a replacement for her driving glasses.

Today on the floor

Ask a colleague for a made-up day (office, driving, reading at night). Name the lens type you would suggest, in plain words and with no product name.

About 3 minutes, with a colleague.
