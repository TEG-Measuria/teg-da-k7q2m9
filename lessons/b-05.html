Road Pilot for drivers

7 minutes · Lenses

Purpose

After this lesson you can describe Road Pilot, say who it suits, and discuss night driving honestly without making promises that lenses cannot keep. It matters because driving is the single most common safety-related need we hear from varifocal wearers.

Worked example

Mr G, 61, a delivery driver, wears varifocals. He says, "I drive about six hours a day. I find it hard to read the dashboard quickly, and at night the headlights seem to flare."

You ask: how far ahead does he look, how often does he check the satnav or dashboard, and does he drive at night? He says mostly motorway, the dashboard and mirrors, and night drives twice a week.

You explain: "A standard varifocal is a good all-rounder. Road Pilot is a varifocal design built with drivers in mind: it widens the area for the road ahead and the middle distances such as mirrors and dashboard, so you do not have to move your head as much. For the headlight flare, the anti-reflection coating helps with reflections inside the lens, but no lens removes glare from bright lights completely. I would also ask the optometrist whether anything else is worth checking, because night glare can have more than one cause." You do not promise that night driving will feel perfect, and you tell him to ring if he is not comfortable.

He chooses Road Pilot, you take his heights and measurements carefully, and note on the dispense that it is for professional driving.

The lesson

Road Pilot (Varilux Road Pilot) is a progressive lens from Essilor made for people who drive a lot. Essilor describes it as widening the distance and middle zones, which are the parts of the lens used for the road, the mirrors and the dashboard. It is a varifocal, so it carries the same adaptation as any progressive and the patient must have a near add.

Who it suits: a varifocal wearer who drives often, for work or in a long commute, and who looks at the dashboard and mirrors as well as the road. It is also a sensible choice for someone who drives and wants one pair to cover everything.

Who needs a different answer: a single vision wearer who only needs distance for driving (single vision is simpler and wider); a patient who wants a screen or desk pair (see b-03 and b-04); a patient with a night-driving complaint that is not about their lenses. A new complaint of glare or poor night vision is something to tell the optometrist about, because it may have a medical cause. You never diagnose; you pass it on.

Night driving and coatings: an anti-reflection coating such as Crizal UV, which comes with Road Pilot on our list, reduces reflections on the lens surfaces and helps with comfort at night. It cannot remove the glare from oncoming headlights, and it is wrong to say it will. Use phrases such as "may help" and "many people find".

Sun and driving: photochromic lenses (b-12) react to UV, and car windscreens block much of it, so they stay lighter in the car. A polarised sun pair (b-13) is the usual answer for daytime driving glare. Do not recommend a tinted lens for night driving.

Fitting matters: heights are measured with the patient wearing the frame in their natural posture. A driving lens that is fitted too high or too low spoils the benefit. Take VisiOffice measurements where possible, and follow the fallback card if not.

Price and bands: Road Pilot exists in every index band on the list, clear or Transitions, so check price-it for the exact cell. For example, at the standard index the clear pair is £250 and the Gold member price is £200.

Watch-outs

  • Never promise that a lens will remove night glare. Say "may help".
  • Do not say a patient is safe or unsafe to drive. The legal side of vision for driving is not our decision; if a patient worries about it, ask the optometrist.
  • Never recommend a tint for night driving.
  • A new or worsening night-vision complaint goes to the optometrist.
  • If unsure, ask a registrant.

Tips

  • Ask "how many hours a week, and what do you look at?" in the first minute.
  • Offer a polarised sun pair for daytime driving as a second pair.
  • Write the driving use on the dispense so the next person knows why Road Pilot was chosen.

Current practice notes

  • Road Pilot is on the group price list in all four index bands; check price-it for the clear or Transitions cell.
  • Essilor describes Road Pilot as a driving-oriented progressive with widened distance and middle zones.

Drill

Out loud: "Mrs B, 57, drives forty minutes each way, wears varifocals and says night driving is worse than it was." Say what you offer, what you will not promise, and who you tell.

Show model answer

Offer Road Pilot with the anti-reflection coating, explaining that it widens the area for the road, mirrors and dashboard. Do not promise that glare will disappear: say the coating may help with reflections. Because the night driving has got worse, tell the optometrist, since it may not be a lens matter.

Today on the floor

Role-play with a colleague who finds night driving harder. Say what you offer and what you will not promise about night driving.

About 4 minutes, with a colleague.

What your coach looks for

  • Names the right product
  • No promise of no glare or safe driving
  • Says to tell a registrant about night driving problems
