Need objections: just the voucher, only for reading, old ones were fine

7 minutes · The conversation

Purpose

You can respond to three "I do not need that" objections: "I just want what the voucher covers", "they are only for reading" and "my old ones were fine". It matters because these patients are not refusing; they are telling you what they think they need, and your job is to check gently whether that is the whole picture.

Worked example

Mr N, 68, retired, holds an NHS voucher. He says, "I just want whatever the voucher covers."

The dispenser acknowledges: "Absolutely, that is completely fine." She explains what the voucher covers: "Your voucher covers a complete pair of glasses at no cost to you, from any frame in this range." She then asks one question: "Do you wear them all day, or just for certain things?" Mr N says all day. She says: "Some people who wear them all day add an anti-reflective coating or a thinner lens. I will show you what it adds, and you decide." Mr N says, "No, the basic will do." She says, "Of course. Shall we start with the frames that are covered?"

She asked once, explained honestly, and accepted the answer. She did not suggest the covered pair was second-rate.

The lesson

"I just want what the voucher covers"

This is a legitimate request. Respect it.

  1. Acknowledge: "Absolutely."
  2. Explain what the voucher covers. Always explain what the voucher covers, so the patient knows what they are getting.
  3. Ask one question about their day, and mention the extras once.
  4. Let them decide. Start with the frames that are covered.

Never suggest the voucher pair is second-rate. Whether a patient is eligible is covered by NHS rules, so do not assume.

"They are only for reading"

This is common and may be exactly right. Ask what else they do: "What else do you look at in a day, a phone, a computer, the TV?" If they only read, say so: "Then a reading pair is just what you need." If they also use a computer or phone, you can mention that reading glasses are set for one distance. Whether another type of lens is right for them is for the optometrist's advice; if unsure, ask a registrant.

Do not tell them reading glasses are harmful. They are not.

"My old ones were fine"

This is partly a statement of loyalty to something that worked. Acknowledge it: "That is great, they have done you well." Then ask: "How long have you had them?" and "Has anything changed, such as glare at night or how clean they look?" Lenses are coated, and the coating wears over time like a tyre wears even when the car runs fine. If the optometrist advised a change, say so. If the prescription has not changed, say, "Great news, your eyes are healthy," and explain what a fresh lens would do for comfort. If they still say no, accept it.

The common thread

In all three cases:

  • Acknowledge first.
  • Ask one clarifying question.
  • Give an honest answer in terms of their day.
  • Check, and close.
  • Accept the no warmly.

Watch-outs

  • Do not suggest the voucher pair is second-rate.
  • Do not push extras on a patient who has told you they only want what is covered.
  • Do not make a patient feel foolish about reading glasses.
  • Do not say "the optometrist advises" unless it is on the record.
  • If unsure whether a different lens is suitable, ask a registrant.

Tips

  • With voucher patients, show the covered frames first and be positive about them.
  • With "only for reading", ask about the phone. Many people squint at it without realising.
  • With "old ones were fine", ask when they were made.

Drill

A patient says, "They are only for reading." Write your two-step response: acknowledge, then ask one question.

Show model answer

"That is common, and it may be just what you need. What else do you look at in a day, a phone, a computer, the TV?" Then listen, and respond to what they say.

Today on the floor

A colleague uses the need objection cards (voucher only, reading only, old ones were fine) to play the patient. Role-play one, swap, then do another. Check gently whether they really only need what they asked for.

About 5 minutes, with a colleague.

What your coach looks for

  • Does not argue
  • Asks about their day before answering
  • Accepts a patient who genuinely needs only what they asked for
  • Never suggests something that is not needed
