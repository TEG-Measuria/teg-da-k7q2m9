Goodwill and complaints

7 minutes · Aftercare

Purpose

You can handle a complaint or a goodwill request calmly and fairly: listen, apologise, take details, pass it to the right person and keep your promises.

Worked example

Mr H, 45, delivery driver, comes back angry: "I paid good money and the frame has snapped at the hinge after three months." You stay calm and say, "I am sorry that has happened. Can you tell me what happened?" He says it snapped when he put them on. You look at the frame. It has a clean break at the hinge.

You do not say "that is not covered" or "we will replace it". You say, "Thank you for telling me. Let me take a few details and speak to the practice manager. I will ring you by end of tomorrow." You write down his words, the date of purchase, the frame details and what you saw. You scan a note to the record and tell the practice manager. You ring him the next day with the answer.

The lesson

A complaint is a chance to show the patient you are on their side. Most complaints are solved by being heard.

The steps

  1. Listen without interrupting. Let them finish.
  2. Apologise for the experience, not for blame: "I am sorry that has happened."
  3. Ask questions to understand: what, when, what happened.
  4. Take details and write them down in the patient's own words.
  5. Say what happens next and by when. Keep that promise.
  6. Pass the case to the right person: practice manager, dispensing optician or optometrist.
  7. Follow up. Ring the patient to check they are happy.

What is goodwill? Goodwill is something the practice gives although it does not have to: a free adjustment, a discount, a repair. The practice manager decides what goodwill is possible; you do not decide on the spot. Some goodwill decisions may need the owner.

What you must never do

  • Argue, blame the patient, the lab or a colleague.
  • Say yes or no to a refund on the spot. Take details and come back; the owner signs off refunds.
  • Promise a remake before the triage.
  • Discuss the case in front of other patients.

Clinical complaints. If the complaint is about seeing, headaches, double vision or discomfort, use the first-line non-tolerance steps and fetch a registrant for the stop signs.

Serious complaints. If the patient mentions legal action, harm or the regulator, stop and tell the practice manager at once.

Data. Keep patient details inside the practice's systems. Do not put them in personal messages.

Watch-outs

  • Do not say "it is not our policy" as the first thing the patient hears.
  • Do not promise anything you cannot control.
  • Never say yes or no to a refund on the spot.
  • Do not take the complaint personally.

Tips

  • Slow your voice and lower the volume when someone is upset.
  • Repeat back the problem so they know you heard.
  • Write down the promised time and put it in the diary.

Current practice notes

  • Handle issues without blame; thank the patient for telling you.
  • Refunds are signed off by the owner; take details and come back.
  • Check with your manager the limit of goodwill a dispenser may offer without asking. Check with your practice manager.

Drill

A patient says their frame broke and demands a replacement now. Role-play the first three things you say with a colleague.

Show model answer

Apologise ("I am sorry that has happened"), ask what happened and listen, then take details and say who you will speak to and when you will ring back. Do not promise a replacement or a refund.

Today on the floor

Role-play a complaint with a colleague who plays an annoyed patient (a made-up story, no real names). Use the seven steps. Swap roles. The colleague notes whether you apologised without blaming, took details and gave a time.

About 5 minutes, with a colleague.

What your coach looks for

  • Listens without interrupting
  • Apologises for the experience, not for blame
  • Says what happens next and by when
  • Passes the case on and never agrees a refund on the spot
