Patient data, confidentiality and two identifiers

7 minutes · Induction

Purpose

After this lesson you can protect patient information, check that you are talking to the right person with two identifiers, and recognise a data request or a breach. It matters because patient records are private, and a mistake can hurt someone.

Worked example

Mrs W phones to ask if her glasses are ready. The dispenser says "Before I look, can I take your full name and date of birth?" Mrs W gives both. The dispenser also checks the postcode on the record, because a name alone is not enough. The glasses are ready. Later a man rings and says he is Mrs W's husband and asks what her prescription is. The dispenser does not give it out; she says "I can only talk to the patient about this, but I can take a message." That one sentence protected a private record.

The lesson

What counts as patient data. Anything that identifies a patient or tells you about their health: name, address, date of birth, prescription, appointment history, photos, and what they bought. It lives on screens, in paper job envelopes, and in your head.

Two identifiers. Before you give information or hand over a job, check two identifiers. Full name and date of birth are the usual pair. Address or postcode is a good second option. Never accept one alone, and never offer the answer for the patient to say "yes" to.

Keeping data safe.

  • Lock the screen when you leave the computer.
  • Do not talk about a patient where others can hear.
  • Keep paper out of public view and put it in the correct place at the end of the day.
  • Never take patient information home, photograph it or message it on a personal phone.
  • Only open a record when you have a reason.

A request for personal data (DSAR). Anyone can ask to see the data we hold on them. They may ask in person, by phone, by letter, by email or on social media. A verbal request is valid. Do not turn them away or say "put it in writing". Tell the practice manager at once. A simple request such as a copy of a prescription is handled as normal business, but still checks identity first. Everything else goes to the named lead. The reply is due within one calendar month.

A data breach. A breach is any time patient data goes to the wrong place or person, or is lost: an email to the wrong address, a job bag left on a bus, a screen left open. Tell the practice manager straight away, on the same day. Do not try to fix it or hide it. Where a breach is likely to risk people's rights and freedoms, the regulator must be told within 72 hours, and the data protection officer decides that, so every hour matters.

Phone and email. Do not give clinical or personal details to a caller you have not identified. Check the email address before sending, and never put a full prescription in an email unless the practice process says it is allowed.

Watch-outs

  • Do not accept a single identifier, and do not read out the patient's details for them to confirm.
  • Do not blame yourself or hide a mistake. A fast report is what helps the patient.
  • Do not say "put it in writing" to someone asking for their data.
  • If unsure, ask your practice manager.

Tips

  • Ask for the date of birth first, then a second item, before you open the record.
  • Tell patients why you ask: "it keeps your record private". They nearly always appreciate it.

Current practice notes

  • Check with your practice manager: who the current DSAR lead is and where the data breach form is kept.
  • Two identifiers are checked at collection as step 8 of the 8-step routine.
  • A request for personal data is answered within one calendar month.

Drill

Someone rings and says "it's Mr H, are my glasses ready?" Write the two questions you ask before you look up the job.

Show model answer

"Can I take your full name and date of birth?" and then a second item, such as the postcode or address on the record. Do not accept a name alone, and do not read his details out for him to confirm.

Today on the floor

Role-play a phone call with a colleague asking 'are my glasses ready?' using a made-up job. Ask the two identifier questions before looking anything up.

About 3 minutes, with a colleague.

What your coach looks for

  • Both identifiers asked before any information is given
  • No patient details said aloud where others can hear
  • Uses a training example, not a real record
