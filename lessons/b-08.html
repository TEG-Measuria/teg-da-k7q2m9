Thickness and weight in the patient's language

6 minutes · Lenses

Purpose

After this lesson you can talk about lens thickness and weight without jargon, ask the questions that reveal what the patient really cares about, and recommend an index honestly. It matters because "will they be thick?" is one of the most common questions, and the answer decides whether the patient trusts you.

Worked example

Mrs Q, 47, a solicitor, picks up a frame and says, "My last glasses were like jam-jar bottoms. I do not want that again." Her prescription is moderately strong.

You do not start with the word "index". You ask, "What bothered you most about the old ones: the look, the weight on your nose, or the glass sticking out of the frame?" She says the look: people noticed her thick edges. You say, "Right, so the look matters most. There are three things that decide it: the strength of your prescription, the size of the frame, and the material. We cannot change your prescription, but we can choose a frame that suits it and a material that is thinner." You show her two frames side by side with demonstration lenses, one small and rounded and one large, and let her see the difference.

She picks the smaller frame. You then explain the material in her words: "This one is thinner at the edge, so the lens looks neater and sits closer to the frame." You tell her the price from the list, and she decides. You have answered her real worry rather than selling a number.

The lesson

Patients rarely care about index. They care about three things, and each has a plain-English name:

  1. How it looks. "Thick edges", "bottle bottoms", "the lens sticking out". This is edge thickness for minus prescriptions, and bulging for plus.
  2. How it feels. "Heavy", "slides down my nose", "leaves marks". This is weight, and also how the frame fits.
  3. How it performs. "Can I see clearly out of the edges?" This links to lens design (see b-09 and b-10).

Use these words with patients. Save "index", "refractive" and "aspheric" for when they ask.

The three levers. Almost every thickness and weight conversation comes down to:

  • the prescription (fixed),
  • the frame size and shape (a smaller, rounder frame shows less thickness), and
  • the material (a higher index is thinner; see b-07).

Start with the lever that costs the least: the frame. Then offer the next index up and say what it adds.

Thin does not always mean light. A thinner lens uses less material, but some higher index materials are denser. So say "thinner" when you mean thinner, and "lighter" only when you are sure. If a patient cares most about weight, ask a colleague or check the product information before promising.

Honest limits. A strong prescription will always be thicker at the edge than a mild one, even in the thinnest material. Say, "This will be much neater than your old ones, though with your prescription the edge will still be thicker than someone with a mild one." Patients trust you more when you do not over-promise.

Gold standard, then budget. The Dispensing Conversation teaches us to say what we would advise first and then work down to the patient's budget, not to start with the cheapest. For a patient who cares about the look, the gold standard is the frame plus the material that gives the neatest result. If the budget does not allow, say, "That is fine, let us get as close as we can." Then compare the next bands on the price list. For example, single vision clear at the standard index and at the next band up are £120 and £165 a pair; always use Price-it for the real combination.

Watch-outs

  • Do not say "you will not notice the edge" unless you can show it.
  • Do not use "thin and light" as if they always go together.
  • Do not blame the patient's prescription; say what can be done.
  • Do not discuss price before the patient has seen the difference.
  • If unsure, ask a colleague or a registrant.

Tips

  • Use demonstration lenses and let the patient hold them.
  • Ask "what bothered you most about your old ones?" first.
  • Offer the frame change before the index change.

Current practice notes

  • Index bands and their prices are on the group price list; price the exact combination with price-it.
  • The Dispensing Conversation module on the lens handover teaches gold standard first, then budget; keep that order.

Drill

A "Mr U, 29, minus 4.50 in each eye" says, "I am scared of thick lenses." In under a minute, say what you ask first and what you offer, in plain words.

Show model answer

Ask what bothers him most: the look, the weight or the fit. Explain that the edge depends on the prescription, the frame and the material. Offer a smaller, rounder frame first, show it with demonstration lenses, then offer the next index up and say what it adds. Be honest that the edge will still be thicker than for a mild prescription.
